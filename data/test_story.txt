Chapter 1 — The Body in the Library

The telegram arrived at Ashford Manor at half past seven on the morning of Wednesday, the fourteenth. Detective Inspector Clara Voss read it twice, tucked it into the pocket of her grey coat, and drove the forty minutes from Millhaven to the village of Drenwick without stopping. By the time she reached the manor's iron gate, the fog had not yet lifted.

The dead man was Edmund Ashford, sixty-two years old, found slumped over the mahogany desk in the east-wing library. The local constable, a ruddy man named Briggs, met her on the steps.

"No sign of forced entry," Briggs said. "Housekeeper found him at six. Coroner says he's been gone since somewhere between midnight and two."

Clara stepped into the library. The room smelled of pipe tobacco and something sharper — bitter almonds, she thought. Edmund Ashford's grey eyes stared at nothing. His right hand still rested near a half-finished letter. She did not touch it.

Three other people had been in the manor that night. Edmund's niece, Petra Ashford, twenty-six, had arrived from London the previous afternoon. His business partner, Roland Fenn, fifty-one, occupied the guest suite on the second floor. And Mira Colston, the housekeeper, sixty, had quarters off the kitchen. No one else had any business being there, Briggs assured her.

Clara noted the time on the mantel clock: seven fifty-one. She noted the half-empty decanter on the sideboard. She noted that one of Edmund's brown shoes was untied.

"I'll need to speak with all three," she said.

---

Chapter 2 — Interviews at the Manor

Clara began with Roland Fenn. He was a compact, careful man with black hair going silver at the temples and green eyes that assessed her steadily. He wore a dark suit even at this hour, and his cufflinks caught the morning light.

"Edmund and I have been partners for nineteen years," Fenn said. "Ashford Fenn Capital. We were arguing last night — yes, I'll admit that freely. He wanted to dissolve the partnership and take the firm public. I disagreed. We shouted. I went to bed at eleven. That is all I know."

"Did anyone else hear the argument?"

"Petra, almost certainly. Her room is directly above the study."

Clara found Petra in the morning room, cradling a cup of tea with both hands. The young woman had blue eyes and a pointed chin, and she looked as if she had not slept at all.

"I heard them arguing," Petra said. "Uncle Edmund and Mr. Fenn. After midnight, actually — I heard a door slam at half past twelve. Then nothing."

"Did your uncle confide in you about the partnership?"

Petra paused. She set down her cup very deliberately. "He told me last month that he had changed his will. Mr. Fenn would no longer inherit a share of the estate if the partnership dissolved."

"I see," Clara said. "Thank you."

She left without telling Petra that she had found no will in the desk — only the unfinished letter, which said: *I have decided to put everything in writing before—* and then stopped.

Mira Colston, the housekeeper, was rolling pastry in the kitchen when Clara arrived. Mira had white hair and steady hands and answered every question in a flat, unhurried voice.

"I went to bed at nine-thirty," Mira said. "I heard nothing. I found Mr. Ashford at six, as I told the constable. I did not go into the library until I had to."

"Did Mr. Ashford seem worried lately?"

"Lately, yes. Since about two weeks ago. He received a letter — I saw the envelope on the hall table — and after that he was not himself."

---

Chapter 3 — The Second Search

That afternoon Clara searched the library a second time. She found, behind a loose panel in the wainscoting, a small green ledger. The ledger contained a column of figures that did not match any publicly filed accounts Clara had seen for Ashford Fenn Capital. There were payments to a name she did not recognize: D. Morrow.

She was sitting cross-legged on the carpet studying the ledger when Petra appeared in the doorway.

"I should tell you something," Petra said. She came in and closed the door behind her. "Mr. Fenn has a scar — a long one — on his left forearm. I noticed it at dinner last night when his sleeve rode up. I don't know if it means anything."

"Can you describe it?"

"Perhaps ten centimetres. Old, but not faded entirely. He flinched when he saw me looking."

Clara made a note. "Anything else?"

Petra hesitated. "Uncle Edmund told me in October that someone had been skimming money from the firm for years."

Clara kept her expression neutral. She had not yet told anyone about the ledger, and she was certain that Edmund, by all accounts a private man, would not have shared that detail casually. She filed it away.

She spent the rest of the afternoon in Millhaven, at the records office, tracing the name D. Morrow. By five o'clock she had a result: Delia Morrow, a former accountant at a firm that had been absorbed by Ashford Fenn Capital eleven years ago.

---

Chapter 4 — An Unexpected Arrival

The following morning — Thursday, the fifteenth — Clara arrived at the manor to find Roland Fenn standing in the entrance hall, coat on, luggage at his feet.

"I need to return to London," he said. "I have a board meeting on Friday."

"I'd prefer you stay," Clara said.

Fenn's green eyes hardened. "I am not under arrest, Inspector."

"No. But I may have questions this afternoon, and it would be inconvenient if you were two hours away."

Fenn removed his coat, slowly, and set it on the hall chair.

Clara found Mira in the garden, cutting back the last of the autumn roses. The fog had finally lifted and the light was thin and cold.

"Tell me about Delia Morrow," Clara said.

Mira's shears stopped mid-cut. A beat. Then she resumed. "I don't know that name."

"She worked for a firm your employer absorbed in 2015. She may have continued to have contact with Mr. Ashford."

"I told you. I don't know the name." Mira went back inside without another word.

That evening, Clara sat in her car outside the manor and reviewed her notes. The bitter-almond smell in the library pointed to cyanide. The coroner had confirmed: Edmund Ashford had been poisoned. The cause of death was potassium cyanide introduced into the whisky decanter. Clara had not released this information publicly. She had told only Constable Briggs and the coroner.

Then she remembered: Petra had said, that morning over breakfast, that it was a terrible thing, to be poisoned in one's own library.

Clara wrote the word *Petra* in her notebook and drew a circle around it.

---

Chapter 5 — The Ledger Explained

Friday the sixteenth. Clara drove back to Millhaven, to Delia Morrow's last registered address — a flat above a dry-cleaner's on Carver Street. The woman who answered the door was sixty-three, with cropped grey hair and an expression that managed to be both wary and resigned.

"I've been waiting for someone to come," Delia said, and stood aside.

The flat was tidy and smelled of coffee. Delia sat at the kitchen table and folded her hands.

"Roland Fenn asked me to run a parallel set of accounts from 2014 onward," she said. "Small amounts at first. Enough that it wouldn't flag an audit. I kept the ledger as insurance."

"The green ledger behind the wainscoting."

Delia nodded. "Edmund found it three weeks ago. He confronted Roland. That's when things became dangerous." She paused. "Mr. Fenn also told Petra about the skimming — he rang her last week, trying to get her on his side. Told her not to tell the inspector."

Clara returned to the manor at two in the afternoon. She found Fenn in the sitting room, and she arrested him. He did not resist. His green eyes were very still.

As the constable led Fenn out to the car, Petra appeared at the top of the stairs. Her brown eyes watched him go.

"Is it over?" Petra asked.

"Nearly," Clara said.

---

Chapter 6 — Resolution

Edmund Ashford had not been afraid of Roland Fenn for long enough. The mistake he made was recording everything — the confrontation, the evidence, the name — in a letter instead of going directly to the police. The letter was never finished. Fenn had walked into the library after midnight, after their argument ended, and laced the decanter.

Clara submitted her report on Saturday the seventeenth.

Three things remained unexplained and always would: who had sent Edmund the letter two weeks before his death, what had been in it, and why Fenn had taken nineteen years to become afraid.

Petra left the manor that same day, Saturday, to return to London.

The will, when the solicitor finally produced it, was straightforward: the estate in trust for charitable purposes, Petra to receive the London flat outright. Fenn received nothing. The old will, the one he thought he would inherit under, had been revoked in September.

On Sunday morning Clara returned to Drenwick one last time, on her own, with no particular purpose. She walked the grounds. The last of the roses had been cut. She found Mira in the kitchen, and she found Petra sitting at the kitchen table, drinking tea.

"I thought you'd gone back to London," Clara said.

"I changed my mind," Petra said.

Mira set a cup of tea in front of Clara without being asked.

"Edmund's age," Clara said, to no one in particular. "Sixty-two."

"Fifty-nine," Mira said quietly. "He was fifty-nine. He always lied about his age to seem more authoritative."

Clara did not write that down. Some things were not evidence. Some things were just grief.

She drove back to Millhaven on a road that had finally dried out, thinking about the fourteen hours it would have taken Fenn to drive from Edinburgh to Drenwick on the night of the murder — fourteen hours for a journey he claimed to have made in four.

Edmund Ashford had been found dead on Wednesday morning. The coroner placed death between midnight and two. Fenn's alibi was that he had been at a business dinner in Edinburgh on Tuesday evening and driven directly to the manor after. He had signed the restaurant bill at nine-fifteen PM on Tuesday.

She had not noted that contradiction until now.

She pulled over and wrote it in her notebook.

Then she drove on.
